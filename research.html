Theses

  • The Fateful Adventures of the Good Soldiers: Loyalty, Desertion, and the Czechoslovak Legions

    Master’s thesis, CERGE-EI Supervisor: Sebastian Ottinger In progress

    Abstract

    Wartime desertion is typically studied in conscript armies, where staying may reflect fear rather than loyalty. This paper proposes to exploit the Czechoslovak Legions — a volunteer force formed from emigrants, prisoners of war, and Habsburg army deserters during the First World War — as a cleaner setting for separating genuine commitment from coercion. The first part of the analysis examines what drove Czechoslovak soldiers to desert the Habsburg army and join the Legions, with nationalist identity as the mechanism of interest alongside those established in the existing literature. The second examines why some soldiers subsequently deserted the Legions themselves. Because enlistment was voluntary, this setting substantially reduces the initial coercion confound and allows a cleaner study of what sustained or weakened commitment. The study draws on two datasets totalling approximately 300,000 individual soldier records not previously used in economic research.

    Only the position paper is available at this stage; the thesis itself is in progress.

  • The Laffer Curve in the Czech Republic

    Bachelor’s thesis Prague University of Economics and Business Supervisor: Pavel Potužák 2025

    Abstract

    This thesis analyses the overall tax system, the personal income tax, and the corporate income tax from a Laffer curve perspective, with the objective of finding a revenue-maximising rate for each. Two modelling approaches are used, the second prompted by non-stationarity in the data. The stationary model puts the revenue-maximising rate at 28.6% for the tax system as a whole, 17.5% for personal income tax, and 27.5% for corporate income tax. The second approach identifies linear relationships between growth rates of tax revenue and of tax rates, estimated by OLS, by 2SLS with instrumental variables, and by GMM. The analysis uses quarterly data from 1996 Q1 to 2023 Q4.

Term papers and projects

  • What Drives the Referees’ Performance? The Effect of Referee Coaches on Referees’ Performance

    Statistics term project, CERGE-EI 2026 To be revisited

    Abstract

    Referees in any sport can substantially influence match outcomes, so the natural aim of every sports association is to maximise referee quality — yet the literature lacks empirical evidence on the effects of training methods, and of coaching in particular. I estimate the effect of cumulative previous observations by a referee coach on the referee’s received grade, using a novel panel dataset with referee, coach, and referee-pair fixed effects, built from public and internal Czech Floorball data together with a Regional Referee Committee dataset constructed from referee coach reports. I find a statistically significant effect with diminishing returns up to the fifth observation: cumulatively, the first five observations improve the grade by 0.24 points, about 30% of a standard deviation in received grades. Five observed matches achieve what otherwise takes three to four years of refereeing experience. Theoretical and practical seminars yield no statistically significant effect.

  • Written in Time: Unsupervised Discovery of Emotional and Stylistic Structure in the Project Gutenberg Corpus

    Machine Learning for Social Scientists, CERGE-EI 2026 To be revisited

    Abstract

    This project explores whether machine learning and quantitative text analysis can identify emotional, stylistic, and thematic patterns in literature across time. Using the Project Gutenberg corpus, I construct a feature-level dataset of books based on emotion scores, stylometric measures, and semantic fields. The analysis first examines descriptive genre-level differences, then residualises the extracted features on decade, country, and genre fixed effects. Clustering and network analysis then map similarities between books and authors in the constructed feature space, and a final section studies temporal change around selected historical shocks, with the First World War as the headline case. Literary texts do appear to contain measurable patterns connected to genre, period, and historical context: the war is associated with increases in expressed fear, anger, and conflict-related vocabulary, and with shifts towards more conflict- and past-oriented clusters. These findings should not be read as strictly causal — the identification strategy remains limited — but the project shows how a large literary corpus can be turned into structured data and used to study literary change at scale.
